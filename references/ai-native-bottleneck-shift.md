# Why You're Still Slow Even With AI: The Bottleneck Has Moved

**Video Summary**: [THIS is Why You're Still Slow Even With AI (The Bottleneck Moved--Here's What to Do About It)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hpDC29JdgjI)

---

## Core Thesis

AI has fundamentally inverted the cost structure of knowledge work. **Execution capacity is no longer scarce.** The bottleneck has shifted, but our work habits remain optimized for a world where building things was expensive.

### The Evidence

- **Anthropic** shipped Co-work (a full product feature) in **10 days with just 4 people**, written entirely in Claude Code, with 60-100 releases daily
- **Cursor** went from $1M to $500M ARR faster than any SaaS company in history
- **Coinbase** engineers report single individuals refactoring or building new codebases in days

Meanwhile, most organizations are still asking for 30-day or 3-month AI implementation roadmaps with phases, milestones, and resource allocation plans—optimizing for a constraint that no longer exists.

---

## The Old vs. New Reality

### Old Reality (Execution Was Expensive)
- Finding good engineers was hard
- Training took years
- Every hour of engineering time was precious
- Planning, specs, PRDs, and approval gates protected execution capacity
- Meetings to align before building made sense
- "Measure twice, cut once" was wisdom

### New Reality (Execution Is Cheap)
- The meeting to discuss a feature takes longer than building it
- The PRD can take longer than the prototype
- The planning process can take longer than shipping three versions
- The email thread for approval takes longer than building the prototype
- Writing a PRD can cost more than shipping the whole thing

---

## Where the Bottleneck Has Moved

When you eliminate a bottleneck, it doesn't disappear—it **relocates**. The new constraints are:

### 1. Clarity
> "Do you actually know what's worth building?"

- You can now build faster than you can think
- PRDs were always a substitute for clarity—a hedge against expensive rework
- The bottleneck was never putting the product on the website; it's knowing what product the customer wants
- The ones who make money will be the ones with clarity about what's worth building

### 2. Ambition
> "Are you swinging hard enough?"

- When shipping required a quarter of engineering time, small bets made sense
- Now you can take 50 swings per year instead of 3-4
- **Your risk is now timidity, not building the wrong thing**
- The danger is building "horseless carriages" instead of envisioning 10x better products
- We need the ambition to see what's truly transformative for customers

### 3. Distribution
> "When everybody can build, product is not the moat."

- Getting your product into people's hands is the moat
- Example: Cognition (makers of Devin) partnered with Infosys instead of building their own distribution—accessing a third of a million person team and global client base
- The technology is the easy part; reaching customers is hard
- Established brands and relationships are distribution advantages

### 4. Relationships
> "You can't vibe code a relationship."

- When capabilities compound quickly and platforms keep shifting, relationships are durable
- This is fractal truth—applies to both companies and individuals
- Technical skills are rapidly becoming a commodity
- Being known as a trusted person to work with is a career advantage
- If you have to turn to someone when skills are commoditized, you turn to someone you trust

## 8 Habits to Break

These are risk-management rituals designed for a world where execution was expensive. They've calcified into defaults even though all unit economics have flipped.

### 1. The Permission Loop
**Old logic**: Doing was expensive, so check before you do. Get buy-in.

**New reality**: Asking for permission takes longer than doing. The email thread for approval takes more time than building the prototype. The Slack conversation to confirm direction takes longer than trying both directions.

**What to do**: Default to doing. Build the rough version. Show it. Ask forgiveness when needed. Leaders need to cast wider visions so teams can ship autonomously against that vision.

### 2. Polish as Procrastination
**Old logic**: You get one shot, so make it count. Don't waste expensive execution on something half-baked.

**New reality**: People spend 80% of their time on the last 20% of quality when the marginal value of polish is dropping quickly.

**What to do**: Ship ugly. The rough version that exists beats the polished version that doesn't. A directionally correct, ambitious idea is enough to get going. You can always improve after shipping.

**Important nuance**: Good thinking isn't going out of style. Polish is becoming a way to avoid getting ideas rapidly in touch with reality. Consumer-facing polish can still be valuable—NotebookLM shipped rough, then polished based on market feedback.

### 3. Meetings as Default
**Old logic**: Get alignment before action. Get everyone in the room so we don't waste expensive execution time.

**New reality**: An hour of six people's time is 6 hours—often enough to just build the thing. Meetings about what to build often don't resolve what to build; they surface opinions, create action items, and create delays.

**What to do**: Replace the meeting with a product demo. Build a rough version and show people instead of scheduling a meeting to discuss it. This is a foundational principle at Cursor—everyone commits code (designers, product, engineering) so ideas meet code directly.

### 4. Structured Waiting
**Old logic**: Coordination was important. Wait for feedback, wait for the sync, wait for someone to unblock you.

**New reality**: Most of what you're waiting for doesn't need to be waited for. You're outsourcing your momentum to other people's calendars. Waiting an hour in 2025 is waiting a prototype.

**What to do**: Stop waiting. Do the next thing while you wait for feedback on the first. Assume the answer is yes. If blocked on a decision, make a provisional decision, communicate it, and keep moving.

### 5. Planning Over Doing
**Old logic**: Measure twice, cut once. Planning is cheap, execution is expensive.

**New reality**: Plans are hedges that often cost more than the product. Plans almost never survive contact with reality.

**What to do**: Cut planning by 90%. Replace planning with learning through prototyping. If you haven't built something in the last couple weeks, you're probably overplanning. Let reality inform the plan instead of trying to predict reality with the plan.

**Key insight**: Prediction has become expensive and luxurious because execution is cheap and more accurate.

### 6. Deck Instead of Demo
**Old logic**: Build consensus with a deck. Walk around, get stakeholder buy-in, workshop the presentation.

**New reality**: All the ritual preparation as a hedge against execution is in question.

**What to do**: Build a working prototype and show that instead of the deck. Why not just do the work? Why not just ship to customers?

### 7. Consensus Before Action
**Old logic**: Get everybody aligned before acting. Distribute accountability through group decisions.

**New reality**: The cost of consensus has 10x-100x'ed. Consensus often wasn't real anyway—people agree in meetings then undermine decisions later.

**What to do**: Let consensus come from results that create alignment. "I tried X and here's what happened" is much more persuasive than "let's agree to try X." Run the experiment first, then everyone aligns when they see what actually works.

### 8. Hoarding Until Ready
**Old logic**: Don't show work until it's complete. Half-finished work wastes other people's time.

**New reality**: Sitting on ideas and drafts means getting feedback late, after you've invested in a direction that might be wrong.

**What to do**: Show work early. Get feedback fast. There's an ego component—be willing to show raw, unfinished work. Finding out you're wrong a week from now is better than a month from now.

**Important nuance**: Half-finished AI slop without thought creates downstream work. Half-finished work *with* thought and direction that needs feedback is different—you're still applying judgment about what's good.

## Addressing Counterarguments

> "Quality matters in my domain. My boss expects a plan. I'll get in trouble if I just do things without asking."

This is fair—habits have different impacts in fields with high legal and compliance risks (medicine, law, finance).

**But ask yourself**:
- How much of the process is actually required vs. just the way things have always been done?
- Most of us have more latitude than we're using
- Habits that feel mandatory are often just defaults nobody has questioned
- If you keep your eye on quality, you can still be compliant while getting there faster

**Start small**: Pick the lowest-stakes habit from the eight and break just that one. Maybe it's shipping without the usual polish, skipping a meeting to build instead, or making a decision instead of waiting for approval.

## Practical Examples

### Old Way: Process Improvement Idea
1. Write up a proposal
2. Schedule a meeting
3. Meeting surfaces questions
4. Update the proposal
5. Weeks later, get approval to try a pilot

### New Way: Process Improvement Idea
1. Spend an afternoon building a rough version
2. Show three people
3. Two have concerns that kill the idea? Good—found out in a day, not a month
4. Or they like it—iterate, pilot, launch

---

### Old Way: Leadership Presentation
1. Spend a week on the deck
2. Refine transitions, wordsmith messages
3. Anticipate objections
4. Present, leadership asks questions

### New Way: Leadership Presentation
1. Spend 20 minutes to 2 hours on a rough deck
2. Send to your boss: "This is rough. Does the direction make sense?"
3. She flags two problems you didn't consider
4. Fix them in 10 minutes
5. Final deck took 90 minutes and is better because you got feedback early

## The Through-Line

All eight habits are risk-management rituals that made sense when doing was expensive. Those unit economics have flipped.

**The risk isn't wasting execution time anymore. It's wasting time on anything that isn't doing.**

- The permission loop costs more than the thing you're asking permission for
- The polish costs more than shipping
- The meeting costs more than the prototype

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **The chaos you feel is the gap** between where the bottleneck has moved and the habits you still have
2. **When you close that gap**, align your work habits to how AI changes scarcity, and things start making sense
3. **The truly precious resources** are still human judgment things: clarity, ambition, distribution, relationships
4. **People who figure this out first** will operate at Anthropic/Cursor velocity, not traditional big company velocity—not because they have better tools, but because they've stopped doing things no longer worth doing

---

## The Challenge

In a chaotic world, recognize that the chaos comes from being **out of sync with where AI is pushing scarcity**.

- Worry less about what execution is enabling companies to do
- Worry more about shifting your work habits
- Practice getting ideas into contact with reality quickly
- Solve real customer problems
- Shift attention to what actually requires good human judgment

**Hard truths**:
- It's hard to get clear on things
- It's hard to be ambitious (we think smaller than we should)
- Distribution is hard to tackle (entrepreneurs overindex on product, underindex on go-to-market)

But these are exponentially more valuable now that execution is cheap.

---

*Stop treating process as a prerequisite. Start treating iteration and getting ideas into contact with reality as the process.*
